### assistant 2026-05-22T19:47:48.261Z

It's **motion in limine** (pronounced roughly *"lim-in-ee"*), not motion for lemonade.

Although "motion for lemonade" would probably improve courtroom morale.

Generally speaking:

- **Motion to Suppress** = "The government obtained this evidence unlawfully, so the jury should never see it."
- **Motion in Limine** = "Even if the evidence exists, the jury should not hear about it (or hear it in a certain way) because it is irrelevant, unfairly prejudicial, speculative, hearsay, improper character evidence, etc."

Law students are often taught to think of it like this:

| Motion | Purpose |
|----------|----------|
| Motion to Suppress | Attack how evidence was obtained |
| Motion in Limine | Attack whether evidence should be presented |
| Motion to Dismiss | Attack legal sufficiency of case |
| Motion for Discovery | Obtain evidence/information |
| Motion to Compel | Force production of evidence |
| Motion for Continuance | Move hearing/trial date |
| Motion for Protective Order | Limit disclosure or conduct |

A generic Motion in Limine framework looks something like:

```text
ARGUMENT I

EVIDENCE OF PRIOR BAD ACTS SHOULD BE EXCLUDED.

The proposed evidence is not relevant to any material issue
before the jury and serves only to invite improper character
reasoning.

Alternatively, any limited probative value is substantially
outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion of
issues, and misleading the jury.

ARGUMENT II

SPECULATIVE TESTIMONY SHOULD BE EXCLUDED.

A witness may testify to personal observations but may not
speculate regarding intent, motive, or matters outside the
witness's personal knowledge.

ARGUMENT III

HEARSAY STATEMENTS SHOULD BE EXCLUDED.

The proposed statements constitute hearsay and do not fall
within a recognized exception to the hearsay rule.
```

In a typical criminal case, lawyers often file a stack of pretrial motions rather than just one giant document:

```text
1. Motion to Suppress Evidence
2. Motion to Suppress Statements
3. Motion in Limine Re: Prior Bad Acts
4. Motion in Limine Re: Hearsay
5. Motion to Compel Discovery
6. Motion for Disclosure of Witnesses
7. Motion for Evidentiary Hearing
8. Motion to Exclude Expert Testimony
```

Whether any particular motion is useful depends entirely on the facts and evidence, which is why attorneys spend so much time on discovery, reports, bodycam footage, witness statements, timelines, and constitutional issues before drafting anything.

So your intuition is basically right: a suppression motion and a motion in limine are often siblings in the same case, but they attack different problems. One says *"this evidence was obtained unlawfully."* The other says *"this evidence shouldn't reach the jury even if it was lawfully obtained."*
